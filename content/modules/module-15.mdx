## The $120,000/Year Question Nobody Asks Until It’s Too Late

Here’s a scenario that plays out in thousands of families every year: a 78-year-old needs help bathing, dressing, and managing medications. The family looks into assisted living. The cost: $6,000-$8,000 per month. They check Medicare. Medicare doesn’t cover it. They check the parent’s savings. Enough for 2-3 years. Then what?

Long-term care insurance exists to answer that “then what.” It covers the costs that Medicare won’t: nursing homes, assisted living, memory care, and in-home care. The catch? If you wait until you need it, you can’t get it.

**This module helps you understand whether LTC insurance makes sense for your situation, what to look for in a policy, and what to do if insurance isn’t an option.**

## What LTC Insurance Covers (and What It Doesn’t)

## What It Covers

•  In-home care (home health aides, personal care assistants)

•  Adult day care

•  Assisted living

•  Memory care

•  Nursing home care

## What It Does NOT Cover

•  Medical care (that’s Medicare/health insurance)

•  Short-term rehab after hospitalization (that’s Medicare Part A)

•  Pre-existing conditions during the waiting period

Benefits are triggered when you can’t perform 2+ Activities of Daily Living (ADLs): bathing, dressing, eating, toileting, transferring (getting in/out of bed), and continence. Or when you have a cognitive impairment requiring supervision.

## Pros, Cons, and the Ideal Buying Window

## Pros

•  Protects savings and investments from being depleted by care costs

•  Gives you choice of care (home, assisted living, nursing home)

•  Reduces the burden on adult children

•  Peace of mind for the entire family

## Cons

•  Premiums are expensive and can increase over time

•  Traditional policies are “use it or lose it” - if you never need care, premiums are gone

•  Strict underwriting - pre-existing conditions can disqualify you

•  Some insurers have left the market, causing disruption for policyholders

## The Ideal Buying Window: Ages 50-65

**During this window you’re more likely to be healthy enough to qualify, and premiums are significantly lower. An identical policy for a 55-year-old couple can cost more than double if they wait until 65. After 70, premiums are often prohibitive, and qualifying becomes difficult.**

## Traditional vs. Hybrid Policies

**Traditional LTC Insurance:** Standalone policy. Regular premiums. If you need care, it pays. If you don’t, premiums are lost. Typically offers the most robust long-term care benefits per dollar of premium.

**Hybrid LTC Insurance:** Combines LTC coverage with life insurance or an annuity. If you need care, you access the death benefit while alive. If you don’t, your heirs get the death benefit. Eliminates the “use it or lose it” concern. Trade-off: requires larger upfront premium and may offer less LTC coverage than a comparable traditional policy.

Both types have a place. Traditional is better for maximizing LTC coverage. Hybrid is better for those who can’t accept the possibility of “wasted” premiums. Use Tool 15B to compare specific policies side by side.

## What to Do If You Can’t Afford or Qualify for LTC Insurance

If LTC insurance isn’t viable, you need an alternative plan. Ignoring the problem is not a strategy.

**Medicaid Planning:** Work with an elder law attorney to structure assets legally for future Medicaid eligibility. Start 5+ years before care is needed due to the look-back period.

**Self-Funding:** Earmark specific assets or investments for long-term care. Know your monthly cost projection and how many years your reserves will last.

**Family Agreements:** Create a formal agreement about how care will be provided and paid for. Include who provides hands-on care, who contributes financially, and what happens when circumstances change.

**Home Equity:** The home is often the largest asset available for care funding. A planned home sale (Module 9) can fund years of senior living.
