## Revocable vs. Irrevocable Trusts

**Revocable Living Trust:** You control it. You can change it anytime. Avoids probate and provides incapacity management. Does NOT protect assets from Medicaid spend-down or creditors. Cost: $2,500-$5,000.

**Irrevocable Trust:** Once created, generally cannot be changed. Assets transferred out are no longer “yours” for Medicaid purposes. Powerful asset protection. Must be funded 5+ years before Medicaid application. Cost: $3,000-$7,000.

**The key difference: a revocable trust protects your family from probate. An irrevocable trust protects your assets from Medicaid and creditors. Most families with significant assets need both.**

## The Medicaid Asset Protection Trust (MAPT)

The MAPT is specifically designed to protect assets while allowing eventual Medicaid eligibility. Here’s how it works:

•  An elder law attorney creates the irrevocable trust

•  You transfer assets (home, savings, investments) into the trust

•  A trustee (typically an adult child) manages the assets

•  You can continue living in your home and receiving trust income

•  After 5 years (the lookback period), assets are no longer countable for Medicaid

**A properly structured MAPT can protect a family’s entire life savings from being depleted by nursing home costs of $100,000+ per year.**

**RYAN'S REAL TALK:** *The MAPT is not a magic trick. It’s a legal tool that works within Medicaid’s rules. But it only works if you plan ahead. I’ve seen families save $300,000+ because they set up a MAPT at age 70 and didn’t need Medicaid until 78. I’ve seen families lose everything because they waited until the crisis to ask about it.*

## Gifting Strategies and Beneficiary Designations

## Gifting

•  Annual gift tax exclusion: $18,000 per person per year (2026) — no gift tax return needed

•  Gifts ARE subject to the 5-year Medicaid lookback regardless of tax implications

•  Transferring a home directly to a child can trigger capital gains tax issues — a trust is almost always better

•  Never gift assets without consulting an elder law attorney first

## Beneficiary Designations

This is the most commonly overlooked element. Beneficiary designations on IRAs, 401(k)s, and life insurance override your will and trust. An ex-spouse, a deceased person, or a minor child as beneficiary creates expensive legal problems.

•  Review ALL beneficiary designations annually

•  Update after any major life event (death, divorce, birth, marriage)

•  Ensure designations align with your trust and will
