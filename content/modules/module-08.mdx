Let me tell you about the most expensive mistake I see families make in senior transitions. It's not overpaying for repairs. It's not choosing the wrong community. It's not even selling the house below market value. It's having no estate plan.

When a senior becomes incapacitated without a power of attorney, everything stops. The family can't pay bills. Can't sell the house. Can't make medical decisions. Can't access bank accounts. The only path forward is a court-supervised guardianship proceeding that costs $5,000-$15,000 and takes 2-6 months. During that time, the mortgage still comes due, the utilities still run, and the senior's care still needs to be paid for.

## The 5 Essential Documents

You met the core documents in Module 06 when you built the legal foundation. This module looks at them again through the estate lens, what they cost, the mistakes that get made, and the fifth item families forget most often.

### 1. Will or Living Trust

A will directs asset distribution after death through probate (court-supervised, public, 6-18 months). A revocable living trust does the same but avoids probate entirely, private, faster, and less expensive in the long run. For most homeowners, a trust is the better tool.

**Will:** $500-$1,500 to create. Goes through probate. Public record. Does nothing during incapacity.

**Trust:** $2,500-$5,000+ to create. Avoids probate. Private. Your successor trustee can manage assets if you become incapacitated. Must be "funded", assets must be retitled into the trust for it to work.

### 2-4. The Powers of Attorney and HIPAA: Your Module 06 Refresher

You built these three in Module 06, so here is just the estate-lens recap. The durable financial power of attorney keeps a trusted agent able to pay bills, manage investments, and handle real estate through incapacity; without it, the family is looking at court-supervised guardianship at $5,000-$15,000. The healthcare power of attorney and living will put medical decisions and end-of-life wishes in writing. The HIPAA authorization keeps providers legally able to talk to the family, and some providers want it as its own document even when a healthcare POA exists. All three expire at death, which is exactly why documents 1 and 5 carry the estate itself.

### 5. Beneficiary Designations

Not a document you create with an attorney, these are on file at your bank, investment company, and insurance provider. Beneficiary designations on IRAs, 401(k)s, and life insurance override your will and trust. An ex-spouse can inherit everything if you forget to update these. Review annually.

**RYAN'S REAL TALK:** The #1 estate planning mistake I see: a family creates a beautiful trust, pays $4,000 for it, and then never retitles their assets into it. A trust that doesn't own anything is an empty bucket. Make sure your attorney walks you through the funding process and verifies that the house, bank accounts, and investment accounts are titled in the trust's name.

## The 5 Most Costly Mistakes

**Mistake 1, Procrastinating:** Once capacity is lost, it's too late to create these documents. A stroke, fall, or sudden illness can happen without warning. Schedule the attorney appointment this week.

**Mistake 2, Using DIY Online Documents:** Online forms are generic, often not state-specific, and frequently executed improperly. An invalid document is worse than no document, it creates a false sense of security.

**Mistake 3, Not Funding the Trust:** A trust that doesn't own assets doesn't protect anything. The house deed, bank accounts, and investments must be retitled into the trust.

**Mistake 4, Outdated Beneficiary Designations:** These override your will and trust. Ex-spouses, deceased individuals, or minor children as beneficiaries cause expensive legal problems.

**Mistake 5, Choosing the Wrong Decision-Makers:** Your executor and POA agents need to be trustworthy, organized, financially responsible, and available. It's not an honorary title, it's a job. Use Tool 8D to evaluate candidates objectively.

## When There Is No Will, or the House Is Still in a Dead Parent's Name

So far this module has been about doing estate planning right. This lesson is about the mess that shows up when it did not happen, because in a lot of families it did not, and the house is where it all comes to a head. This is one of the most common problems I get called about, and one of the most preventable.

### Dying Without a Will (Intestacy)

If someone dies without a will, the state decides who gets what, using a fixed formula that has nothing to do with what your parent actually wanted. It usually splits everything among the surviving spouse and children in set shares. That sounds tidy until it lands on a house. Now several people own pieces of one home, and none of them can sell, refinance, or even fix it up without all the others agreeing. That is how a paid-off family home turns into a source of family war.

### Heirs' Property, the Trap Nobody Sees Coming

When a home passes down this way, especially over a couple of generations where nobody ever did the paperwork, it becomes what is called heirs' property. A dozen cousins might each own a sliver of one house without realizing it, and no single person holds clear title. Here is why that is dangerous: any one of those owners, or an investor who buys out one of them for cheap, can force the entire property to be sold through a court partition action, often for a fraction of its worth. Speculators go looking for exactly these tangled-title homes, buy one relative's share, and use it to pry the whole property loose. Families have lost land that was theirs for generations this way.

### The House Still in a Dead Parent's, or Grandparent's, Name

Here is the version I see most: a parent passed years ago, the family kept paying the taxes and living in or holding the house, but nobody ever moved the title out of the deceased person's name. Now the family wants to sell, or borrow against it, or just protect it, and they cannot, because legally they do not own it yet. The estate was never settled. Until that title is cleared, the family is stuck, and the property is wide open to anyone who shows up claiming a piece of it.

### How You Fix It

The fix is almost always a probate or estate proceeding to move the title into the living heirs' names, and depending on the state and the size of the estate, there may be a simpler small-estate or affidavit process. This is attorney work, an estate or probate attorney, not something to handle with an online form. If the title is tangled across many heirs, a real estate attorney may also need to do what is called a quiet title action to clean it up. It costs something, but it is the only way to turn a house the family cannot touch into one they actually own and can sell or protect.

And if selling that home is where the family lands once the title is clear, that is the exact moment to be careful with the biggest check of the whole process. Tell me the house and the situation, and I will help you find and vet an agent who has actually handled inherited and dated homes like yours, or tell you straight if a cash sale or holding it makes more sense. It costs your family nothing, the agent pays the referral, not you. I have been on the other side of these deals, and now I am on yours.

**IF SOMEONE CLAIMS THEY OWN THE HOME:** If a stranger, an investor, or even a relative shows up with papers claiming they own or bought a piece of the home, do not sign anything on the spot. A real change of ownership, or a real order for anyone to leave, comes through the county records and the courts, not a knock at the door. Get the paperwork in front of a real estate or estate attorney before you act on it. If a situation ever escalates, cooperate with law enforcement and any genuine court order, and let your attorney settle the ownership question through the proper process. Keep the deed and the death certificate somewhere safe.

## The Tax Nobody Warns You About: Step-Up in Basis

This one is a tax rule, but stay with me, because getting it wrong can cost your family tens of thousands of dollars, and getting it right is often free.

When someone inherits a house after the owner dies, the IRS resets the home's cost basis to its value on the date of death. That is the step-up in basis. In plain terms: if your parent bought the house for $60,000 decades ago and it is worth $300,000 when they pass, the heirs' starting point for taxes becomes $300,000, not $60,000. If they sell it soon after for around $300,000, there is little or no capital gains tax on all that appreciation. Decades of growth, wiped clean, just by inheriting it.

### Why Gifting the House During Life Can Backfire

Here is the mistake families make with good intentions: to avoid probate or a nursing home, a parent deeds the house to the kids while they are still alive. But a gift during life usually carries the parent's original low basis with it. Using the same numbers, the kids take a $60,000 basis instead of a $300,000 one, and when they sell, they can owe capital gains tax on nearly a quarter million dollars of gain that a simple inheritance would have erased. There can be good reasons to transfer a home during life, but the tax cost is real, and it should be a decision made with a CPA and an attorney, not a kitchen-table move to dodge probate.

**RYAN'S REAL TALK:** I am not a CPA, and this is exactly the kind of thing you bring one in for. But I have watched families hand the house to the kids early thinking they were being smart, and hand them a tax bill in the process that dwarfed anything probate would have cost. Before anyone signs a deed to move a house around, to the kids, into or out of a trust, or to qualify for anything, get a CPA and an attorney to look at the tax side first. Free advice up front beats a surprise at the closing table.

**EDUCATION, NOT TAX ADVICE:** Step-up in basis and gift-tax rules are federal, can change, and your situation may differ. Confirm the numbers with a CPA or tax professional before you make any move.

## Digital Assets and Your Estate

Your digital life is part of your estate. Email accounts, social media profiles, online banking, cloud-stored photos, subscriptions, and cryptocurrency. Without a plan, your family may be locked out of accounts containing irreplaceable photos, financial records, or ongoing subscriptions that keep charging.

### What to Include in Your Digital Asset Inventory

- Email accounts (Gmail, Yahoo, Outlook)
- Social media (Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn)
- Online banking and financial accounts
- Cloud storage (Google Drive, Dropbox, iCloud)
- Subscription services (Netflix, Amazon, streaming)
- Digital photo libraries
- Password manager credentials (if you use one)
- Cryptocurrency wallets and keys

Store this inventory securely. Tell your executor exactly where to find it. Update it annually. Use Tool 8B for the complete template.

## Choosing the Right Decision-Makers

The people you choose for these roles will have enormous power over your finances, healthcare, and legacy. Choose carefully.

### Executor / Successor Trustee

Manages the estate after death. Pays debts, distributes assets, files final tax returns. Must be organized, detail-oriented, and able to handle pressure from other family members. Choosing the oldest child by default is a mistake if they're not the most capable.

### Financial Power of Attorney Agent

Manages finances during incapacity. Access to bank accounts, authority to pay bills and manage investments. Must be trustworthy with money and available to act quickly when needed. Consider naming a professional fiduciary if no family member is suitable.

### Healthcare Power of Attorney Agent

Makes medical decisions during incapacity. Must understand the senior's wishes, be willing to advocate with doctors, and be emotionally capable of making difficult end-of-life decisions.

The best executor might be your youngest child. The best healthcare agent might be a trusted friend who happens to be a nurse. Choose based on capability, not obligation.
