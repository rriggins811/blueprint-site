**The Move Is Done. The Transition Isn’t.**

Congratulations, the house is sold, the move is complete, and the senior is in their new home. Most families exhale at this point and think the hard part is over.

It’s not. The next 90 days are the most critical period of the entire transition. Research shows that seniors who don’t establish social connections and comfortable routines within the first three months are significantly more likely to experience depression, cognitive decline, and a desire to “go back home.”

**This module is about turning a new address into a home. It’s about routine, connection, and monitoring, the unglamorous but essential work that determines whether this transition succeeds long-term.**

## The First 72 Hours: Creating a Sanctuary

The goal of the first three days is comfort, safety, and stability, not unpacking every box. Focus on creating a sanctuary.

## Day 1: Safety and Rest

**Bedroom:** Make the bed with familiar linens. Set up nightstand with lamp, clock, phone, and water. This is the retreat when everything else feels unfamiliar.

**Bathroom:** All medications accessible. Non-slip mats in place. Towels hung where they’re easy to find. Test water temperature.

**Comfort:** Place 2-3 familiar items where the senior will see them first thing, a favorite photo, a familiar blanket, a cherished decoration.

## Day 2: Functionality

**Kitchen:** Coffee maker working. Basic dishes and utensils accessible. Familiar snacks stocked. This is about normalcy.

**Living Area:** Favorite chair positioned with a good view. TV set up and working. A few more photos and familiar items placed.

## Day 3: Orientation

**Community Orientation:** Walk through the building together. Find the dining room, mailbox, laundry, activity room. Meet at least one neighbor.

**Local Services:** Locate the nearest pharmacy, grocery store, and urgent care. Program these into the senior’s phone.

**RYAN'S REAL TALK:** *The single most effective thing I’ve seen on Day 1 is a daughter who brought her mother’s coffee maker and favorite mug from the old house, set them up on the counter, and had coffee ready when Mom walked in. Mom sat down, drank her coffee in her favorite mug, and said “this isn’t so bad.” That’s the power of familiar objects in an unfamiliar space.*

## Building a New Routine

Routine is the anchor that provides stability in a sea of change. For seniors, especially those with cognitive decline, a predictable daily rhythm reduces anxiety and builds confidence.

## Morning Routine

• Consistent wake-up time

• Breakfast at the same time (or at the community dining room)

• Medications at the same time each day

• One planned morning activity (walk, reading, phone call)

## Afternoon Routine

• Lunch at a consistent time

• One community activity or personal interest

• Rest period if needed

• Phone call or visit with family

## Evening Routine

• Dinner at a consistent time

• Calming wind-down activity (TV, reading, music)

• Evening medications

• Consistent bedtime

**Use Tool 12D (New Routine Builder) to map out the daily and weekly schedule. Post it somewhere visible in the apartment.**

## Encouraging Social Connection

Loneliness and social isolation are as dangerous to senior health as smoking 15 cigarettes a day. That’s not an exaggeration, it’s published research. Social connection is not optional for long-term health.

## How to Help Without Pushing

• Review the community activity calendar together, find 1-2 low-pressure events to try

• Attend the first few activities with the senior so they’re not walking in alone

• Introduce them to neighbors. A simple “Mom, this is \[name\], she lives down the hall” can start a friendship.

• Celebrate small social wins, “You went to the book club! How was it?”

• Don’t force it. Some seniors need weeks or months to feel ready. Respect their pace.

• Use SeniorSafe’s “I’m Okay” check-ins and AI assistant to keep daily connection between visits

**RYAN'S REAL TALK:** *The families who check in daily, even just a 2-minute phone call or a text through SeniorSafe, see dramatically better adjustment outcomes than families who visit weekly. Frequency matters more than duration. A daily “hey Mom, how was lunch?” beats a monthly three-hour visit.*

## Warning Signs and the 30-60-90 Day Check-Ins

## Watch for These Warning Signs

• Withdrawal from activities or family contact

• Changes in eating or sleeping patterns

• Increased confusion or memory issues

• Expressions of regret or wanting to “go home”

• Physical symptoms without medical cause (headaches, stomach issues)

• Refusing to leave the apartment

• Increased irritability or crying

**If you notice 2+ signs persisting for more than 2 weeks, increase contact, talk openly about feelings, consult their doctor, and consider a geriatric care manager.**

## The 30-60-90 Day Framework

**30 Days:** How is the routine working? Any safety concerns? Are social connections starting to form? Any adjustment challenges?

**60 Days:** Is the senior feeling more settled? What adjustments are needed to the space or routine? Is the care level appropriate?

**90 Days:** Overall satisfaction? Long-term adjustments needed? Is this the right community? What’s working and what isn’t?
