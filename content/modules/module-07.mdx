## The Decision That Costs $48,000-$180,000 Per Year

Choosing a senior living community is a financial decision that will cost between $48,000 and $180,000 per year, every year, for the rest of the senior’s life. That makes it one of the single largest financial commitments a family will ever make, larger than most mortgages.

And yet, most families choose based on a 45-minute tour where a marketing director showed them the nicest model apartment, took them past the activity calendar, and pointed out the restaurant-style dining room. That’s not a decision-making process. That’s a sales presentation.

**This module turns you into an informed buyer. You’ll know exactly what questions to ask, which answers are red flags, what costs are hidden, and how to compare communities objectively instead of emotionally. The difference between a good community and a bad one isn’t always visible on a tour. But it’s very visible on your bank statements and in your parent’s quality of life.**

**RYAN'S REAL TALK:** *I’ve seen families sign contracts at communities that seemed perfect during the tour, only to discover hidden fee escalations, understaffed night shifts, and discharge policies that force a move if the senior’s care needs increase. One family was paying $6,500/month and found out that medication management was an extra $800/month, nobody mentioned that during the tour. Ask the hard questions before you sign. That’s what this module is for.*

## The 4 Types of Senior Living

**Independent Living ($2,000-$5,000/month):** For active seniors who don’t need daily help but want community, convenience, and freedom from home maintenance. Includes meals, housekeeping, activities, and transportation. No medical care included.

**Assisted Living ($4,000-$8,000/month):** For seniors who need help with daily activities, bathing, dressing, medication management, but don’t require 24/7 medical care. Includes everything in independent living plus personal care assistance and health monitoring.

**Memory Care ($5,000-$10,000/month):** For seniors with Alzheimer’s, dementia, or significant cognitive decline. Specialized care, secure environment (locked doors), and staff trained specifically in memory care. The secure environment is critical, wandering is one of the most dangerous behaviors in dementia.

**Skilled Nursing / Nursing Home ($8,000-$15,000/month):** For seniors who require round-the-clock medical care and supervision. Physical therapy, wound care, complex medication management, and all personal care. Often used after hospitalization or for complex medical needs.

Many communities offer multiple levels of care on one campus, called “continuing care retirement communities” (CCRCs). The advantage: the senior can move between care levels without leaving the community. The potential downside: CCRCs often require large entrance fees ($100,000-$500,000+) in addition to monthly costs.

**RYAN'S REAL TALK:** *Don’t choose a care level based on where the senior is today. Choose based on where they’ll likely be in 2-3 years. If your parent is in early cognitive decline, a community with both assisted living and memory care means they won’t have to move again when care needs increase. Moving twice is twice the stress and twice the cost.*

## Understanding the True Cost

## What’s Typically Included in the Monthly Fee

• Apartment or room

• Utilities (usually except phone, cable, internet)

• Meals (1-3 per day depending on community)

• Housekeeping and linen service

• Social activities and programming

• Transportation to appointments and shopping

• Maintenance and building upkeep

## What Typically Costs Extra

• Personal care services (bathing, dressing, medication help), $500-$2,000+/month

• Memory care or higher levels of care, $1,000-$3,000+/month above base

• Medication management, $300-$800/month

• Incontinence supplies, $100-$300/month

• Beauty salon services

• Guest meals

• Cable and internet, $50-$150/month

• Pet fees (if allowed), $25-$50/month

**The base rate you see on the brochure is almost never the total cost. A $4,500 base rate can easily become $6,500-$7,500 once personal care, medication management, and other add-ons are included.**

**CRITICAL:** Always ask for a complete fee schedule in writing. Ask specifically: “What would the total monthly cost be for a resident who needs help with bathing, dressing, and medication management?” That’s the real number. Use Tool 7A to calculate and compare.

## How Medicare and Medicaid Apply to Senior Living

This builds on Module 6’s coverage gap lesson, now applied specifically to senior living costs.

## Medicare and Senior Living

Medicare does NOT cover independent living, assisted living, memory care, or long-term nursing home stays. It covers short-term skilled nursing (up to 100 days after a 3-day hospital stay) for rehabilitation, home health care if homebound, and hospice care. That’s it.

## Medicaid and Senior Living

Medicaid covers skilled nursing home care in all states. Some states cover assisted living through waiver programs, but this varies enormously. Medicaid does not typically cover independent living, entrance fees, or private rooms.

**The common path: families pay privately for 2-5 years, spend down assets to Medicaid eligibility ($2,000), then transition to Medicaid coverage. If you’re on this path, make sure the community you choose accepts Medicaid, many don’t, which means another forced move at the worst possible time.**

**RYAN'S REAL TALK:** *Here’s the question nobody asks during a tour but everyone should: “Do you accept Medicaid, and if so, what happens to my parent’s room and care level when they transition from private pay to Medicaid?” Some communities accept Medicaid residents but move them to a different wing, different room, or different care level. Find out before you sign.*

## Reading the Contract: Where the Real Money and Risk Live

The tour is the sales pitch. The contract is the actual deal, and it is where families get hurt, because almost nobody reads it with the right eyes. Everything a smiling marketing director tells you is only as good as what the contract says in writing. Before anyone signs, get the full contract in front of an elder law or real estate attorney. It is a few hundred dollars against a six-figure decision. Here is what to look for so you know what you are reading.

## **Entrance Fees, and Whether You Ever See That Money Again**

Continuing care communities often charge a large entrance fee, anywhere from $100,000 to $500,000 or more, on top of the monthly cost. That money comes in three flavors, and the difference is enormous. A fully refundable fee costs more upfront but returns most of it to the family later. A declining-balance fee shrinks a little every month until, after a few years, there is nothing left to refund. A non-refundable fee is gone the day you pay it. And here is the catch nobody explains at the tour: even a refundable fee is usually not paid back until the unit is re-occupied by the next resident, which can take many months after your parent leaves or passes. Ask in writing: how much is refundable, on exactly what schedule, and does the refund depend on the apartment being filled again.

## **The Three Contract Types**

Continuing care contracts generally come in three types. Type A, sometimes called life care, has the highest entrance and monthly fee but largely locks in your future care costs even as needs increase. Type B includes a set amount of care, then charges discounted or market rates after that. Type C has the lowest entrance fee but you pay full market price for care as you need it, which carries the most financial risk if heavy care comes. None is automatically right or wrong. The mistake is not knowing which one you signed.

## **What Triggers a Forced Move, and Who Decides**

This is the clause families never think to check. Most contracts give the community's own medical staff, not the family, the authority to decide when a resident must move to a higher level of care, or must leave altogether. That means the community can move Mom from assisted living to memory care, at a higher cost, on their call. Ask: who makes that decision, what does the family get to weigh in on, is there an appeal, and under what conditions can a resident be discharged entirely. Get the level-of-care-change and discharge language in writing before you sign, not after.

## **How Fast the Fees Climb**

The monthly fee is not fixed. It goes up, almost every year. Ask for the actual rate-increase history for the last three to five years, in writing. A community that dodges that question is telling you something. To see why it matters: a 5 percent annual increase turns a $6,000 monthly fee into about $7,650 in five years, and that is on top of any care add-ons. Budget for the climb, not just the starting number.

## **The Medicaid Question, in Writing**

If there is any chance the senior outlives their money, this is the most important question in the whole contract: does the community accept Medicaid, and after how many years of private pay? Many require two to three years of private pay before they will keep a resident on Medicaid. Some do not take it at all, which means a discharge at the exact moment the money runs out and the family is least able to handle another move. Do not accept a verbal yes. Get the private-pay requirement and the Medicaid terms in the contract.

## **The Pet Policy**

If your parent has a pet, get the pet policy in writing before anyone falls in love with the place. Size and weight limits, deposits and monthly fees, and the big one nobody asks: what happens to the pet if your parent can no longer care for it. For a lot of seniors that animal is the reason to get up in the morning. This is not the place to rely on a friendly verbal sure, that's fine.

## **Deposits and Waitlists**

Holding deposits and waitlist fees are common, and they are not always refundable. Before you write that check, ask whether you get it back if you change your mind, or if the senior's health changes before move-in. Get the refund terms in writing first.

**RYAN'S REAL TALK:** I have watched one family lose a six-figure entrance fee to a declining-balance clause nobody ever read to them, and another get told two years in that Mom had to leave because the place did not take Medicaid and her money had run out. Both of those were sitting right there in the contract. Both were avoidable. The tour is where they sell you. The contract is where they have got you. Never sign one without an attorney who reads these for a living.

## The 10 Essential Questions for Every Tour

Print Tool 7B and bring it to every tour. These questions reveal what the marketing brochure won’t tell you.

**1. What is your monthly base rate, and what exactly does it include?** Get this in writing. The brochure number is the starting price, ask what a realistic total looks like for someone who needs care.

**2. What are your move-in costs?** Deposits, community fees, first/last month’s rent. Some communities charge $5,000-$10,000+ upfront.

**3. How do you handle care level changes?** If the senior needs more care, how much do costs increase? Can they stay in the same apartment? This is where surprise costs hide.

**4. What is your staff-to-resident ratio?** Lower is better. 1:8 is excellent. 1:15+ is a concern. Ask about night shift ratios, they’re typically worse.

**5. Can I see the specific apartment available?** Not the model. The actual unit the senior would live in. Condition differences between models and available units tell you a lot about maintenance quality.

**6. Can I speak with current residents and families?** A confident community encourages this. Ask residents what they like most and what they wish was different.

**7. What is your staff turnover rate?** Over 50% annually is a red flag. High turnover means inconsistent care and staff who don’t know your parent.

**8. How do you handle medical emergencies?** What’s the protocol for a fall? How quickly is the family notified? Is there 24/7 nursing staff on site?

**9. Can I review the contract before making a deposit?** If the answer is no, walk away. Always have an elder law attorney review the contract before signing.

**10. What is your move-out/discharge policy?** Under what conditions can a resident be asked to leave? This protects against forced moves when care needs increase.

## Red Flags to Watch For

These are the warning signs that a community may not be what it appears. Any one of these should give you serious pause.

**High-Pressure Sales Tactics:**“Sign today for a special discount” or “This apartment won’t be available tomorrow.” A good community doesn’t pressure. A desperate one does.

**Unpleasant Odors:** Persistent urine, mildew, or heavy air freshener masking something. This indicates poor cleaning protocols or understaffing on incontinence care.

**Disengaged Residents:** Most residents sitting alone in rooms or staring at TVs. This means the activities program is inadequate or nonexistent. Social isolation accelerates cognitive decline.

**Unhappy or Overwhelmed Staff:** Pay attention to body language. Stressed, rushed, or unfriendly staff will not provide gentle, patient care to your parent.

**Vague Answers:** If the marketing director can’t give straight answers on pricing, staffing, or policies, the community is hiding something. Transparency is a baseline requirement.

**Won’t Show the Actual Unit:** Only showing model apartments is a red flag. The condition gap between the model and available units reveals maintenance standards.

**Contract Requires Deposit Before Review:** Never sign or pay anything before you and your attorney have read the full contract. Any community that requires this is not acting in your interest.

**Recent Ownership Changes:** New management often means instability, staff turnover, and potential changes to services and pricing. Research the new ownership.

**Trust your gut. Tour the community at different times of day. Show up unannounced for lunch. The community you see during a scheduled tour and the one that exists at 7 PM on a Tuesday are often very different.**

## Comparing Communities and Involving the Senior

## The Scorecard Method

After touring 3-5 communities, they start to blur together. The Comparison Scorecard (Tool 7D) prevents this. Rate each community 1-5 across key criteria: cleanliness, staff friendliness, resident happiness, food quality, activities, location, and value for cost. The scores cut through emotional reactions and give you an objective comparison.

Always eat a meal at each community. The food tells you more about the community’s investment in residents than any brochure. If lunch is institutional and bland, expect everything else to match.

## Involving the Senior

• Let them choose which communities to tour from your pre-screened list, don’t drag them to places they’ve already rejected

• Tour together as a family. The senior should experience it firsthand.

• Focus on positives: social opportunities, restaurant dining, freedom from home maintenance, activities they’d enjoy

• Limit tours to 2 per day maximum. It’s physically and emotionally exhausting.

• Ask the senior what mattered most after each tour. Their priorities may surprise you.

**RYAN'S REAL TALK:** *The senior’s opinion matters more than yours. I know that sounds obvious, but I’ve watched families choose communities based on proximity to the adult children’s house, not the parent’s preferences. Your parent is the one who’s going to live there every day. If they don’t feel good about it, the transition will fail. Let them lead.*
