## The 6 Ways to Sell

Most agents know one way to sell: list it on the MLS and hope. You have six.

**The two most families use**

**Traditional MLS listing.** The highest potential price. It takes prep, showings, and 60 to 90 days. Best for a home in decent shape when you have some time.

**As-is cash offer.** Speed and simplicity. No repairs, no showings, close in 7 to 21 days. Usually 70 to 85 percent of market value. Best for an urgent timeline or a home that needs major work.

About 95 percent of senior transition families use one of those two. The next four are powerful, and in the right situation they can put a lot more money in your pocket, but they are advanced. They are not do-it-yourself, and done wrong they create real risk. Each one needs the right professionals and the right guardrails, and I will tell you exactly who and what.

**The advanced four (right situation, right team only)**

**Owner financing.** You become the bank. Instead of the buyer getting a mortgage, they pay you directly, every month, for years. It can create a steady income stream and often a higher total price. A common structure is a 30-year amortization with a balloon payment due in 5 to 7 years: good monthly income now, the balance paid off or refinanced later. The risk is real, if the buyer stops paying, you may have to foreclose and take the house back. Guardrails: a real estate attorney writes the note and deed of trust, you vet the buyer's down payment and credit, and you go in knowing your risk tolerance. This is never a handshake deal.

**Lease-option (rent-to-own).** You rent the home to a tenant who has the option to buy later, which brings in income while you wait for a better market or a better-qualified buyer. The catch: you are a landlord in the meantime, and a poorly written option can tie up your home for a buyer who never actually buys. Guardrails: an attorney writes the lease and the option separately and correctly, and you screen the tenant like you mean it.

**1031 exchange.** If the home is an investment property, you can defer all the capital gains tax by rolling the proceeds into another property. The savings can be large. The rules are strict and unforgiving: you must identify a replacement within 45 days and close within 180, and you cannot touch the money in between. This one is not optional on the professional side. It requires a qualified intermediary, a 1031 exchange specialist, engaged before your sale closes to hold the funds and run the exchange, plus your CPA on the tax side. Miss a deadline and the whole tax bill comes due.

**Keep it as a rental.** Hold the home, rent it out, and build long-term income and wealth. This makes sense when the home cash-flows and someone can manage it. You are a landlord, so factor in vacancies, repairs, management, and the fact that this ties up equity you might need for care. Guardrails: run the real numbers with a financial advisor, and know that if Medicaid may enter the picture, how and when you move a property matters a great deal (covered in the legal and financial modules).

Knowing all six means you are never forced into a box that does not fit. But hear me on the advanced four: the reason they can make you more money is the same reason they can hurt you, they are complex. Do not run one off a video or a stranger's advice. On these, the right attorney, CPA, 1031 specialist, or financial advisor is not an expense, it is the guardrail that keeps a smart move from turning into an expensive mistake.

## Traditional vs. As-Is: The Real Comparison

**Traditional MLS listing**

- Highest sale-price potential
- Full market exposure, multiple offers possible
- Requires prep work and the Smart Prep Package
- Showings can be disruptive to the senior
- 60 to 90 days, prep plus listing plus closing
- Subject to inspection, appraisal, and buyer-financing contingencies

**As-is cash offer**

- No repairs, no cleaning, no showings
- Leave unwanted items behind
- Close in 7 to 21 days
- A guaranteed close, no financing contingency
- Lower price, usually 70 to 85 percent of market value
- Minimal closing costs, $500 to $1,500

**The hybrid**

Do light prep and list traditionally while collecting cash backup offers at the same time. If the listing does not produce in 30 to 45 days, you have a guaranteed cash exit ready to go.

## Which Repairs Actually Pay You Back

Not every repair is worth doing, and the difference is real money. Before you spend a dollar, sort every project into three buckets: the work that earns it back at resale, the work that is lifestyle-only (nice, but a buyer will not pay extra for it), and the money-losers you will never recover.

On a dated home you do not do a full gut. You do the handful of things that move the number: pull the ruined carpet and refinish the hardwoods underneath, fresh paint, reglaze a dated tub, clean up the yard and the curb. Nothing fancy, just the work that pays back. Spending $60,000 to chase a $30,000 bump is a classic loss, and grief and pressure push families into it every day.

One more that matters: the roof and the HVAC. A buyer cannot easily insure or finance a house with a failing roof, so that one issue can shrink your buyer pool and your price. Get real bids and decide on purpose, fix it before listing, or price for it. Do not guess.

## The Decision Pyramid

Work through these five questions in order. Each answer narrows your path.

**Level 1, timeline.** How fast do you need the funds? Under 45 days points to as-is. Six months or more gives traditional time to work.

**Level 2, home condition.** Light cosmetic work? Traditional. $15,000-plus in repairs? As-is or hybrid.

**Level 3, stress tolerance.** Can the senior handle showings and strangers in the home? If not, as-is removes that entirely.

**Level 4, financial priority.** Is top dollar critical to fund care, or is certainty and speed worth more?

**Level 5, available support.** Do you have local family to manage contractors and showings? If not, as-is or a move manager.

## Calculating Net Proceeds

Never compare sale prices. Always compare net proceeds, what you actually walk away with after every cost.

**Traditional sale costs**

- Real estate commission, 5 to 6 percent
- Repairs and updates, $5,000 to $15,000
- Staging, $1,000 to $3,000
- Closing costs, $2,000 to $5,000
- Carrying costs while on market: mortgage, utilities, insurance, every month

**Cash sale costs**

- Closing costs, $500 to $1,500
- No repairs, no staging, no carrying costs

And do not forget the payoff and the taxes, because they come out of your net too. Any mortgage balance and any HELOC get paid off at closing. And taxes: a primary home has a capital-gains exclusion, up to $250,000 for a single owner and $500,000 for a married couple, but a home owned for decades in an appreciated area can go past that, and a rental carries depreciation recapture. Fold these in with your CPA before you count the money as spent.

Example: a $300,000 home. Traditional net after all costs, about $270,000. A cash offer at 80 percent, about $240,000 net. The gap is about $30,000. The real question is whether that $30,000 is worth 60 to 90 days of showings, repairs, and stress. For some families, yes. For others, absolutely not.

## Two Ways Families Get Taken

There are two ways to lose money on this sale, and you need to see both coming.

**The cash buyer or wholesaler.** The play is always the same: lowball what the house is worth, inflate what the repairs cost, and rush you to sign before you can call an agent. Many "we buy houses" companies are wholesalers who never even own the home. They put it under contract cheap, then sell that contract to a real investor for more, and the gap is their payday. You never see it, and you often net far less than a legitimate cash buyer would have paid. Protect yourself:

- Watch for "and/or assigns" next to the buyer's name. That means they can sell your contract to someone else. That is a wholesaler.
- Ask the three questions a real buyer answers in seconds: Are you the end buyer, or will you assign this to someone else? Can you send me proof of funds today? Are you a wholesaler or the buyer?
- Get 2 to 3 offers so a lowball is obvious, and compare net, not the headline number.
- Never sign a short contract without a real estate attorney reading it, and never accept an assignment clause you do not understand.

A handful of states have started to regulate wholesalers, mostly by requiring them to hold a real estate license. Oklahoma did it in 2021, Illinois in 2019. North Carolina does not regulate wholesalers specifically yet. A bill that would, House Bill 797, would make residential wholesaling licensed activity and give home sellers a right to cancel. It passed the North Carolina House unanimously in 2025, then stalled in the Senate, so as of now it is not law and NC families have no statutory protection here.

Know this, though: a law on the books is a floor, not a guarantee. This is a grey area, and some wholesalers, and even some of the title companies and attorneys working alongside them, will push whatever they can get away with. A common dodge is a "novation," where instead of openly assigning your contract they quietly rewrite it to swap in the real buyer, which can sidestep the rules entirely. Others just ignore the law and bet you will not know the difference. So the real protection is not the statute, it is you knowing your rights and refusing to be rushed: get everything in writing, have a real estate attorney read it before you sign, and slow the whole thing down. Where there is no law, and even where there is, the protection is you.

**The wrong agent.** Nobody warns you about this one. The neighbor who has sold a few houses, the relative who got licensed last year, or the agent who only sells new construction often cannot price a dated home or know the handful of repairs that actually raise the sale price. They underprice it, or they talk you into a big remodel chasing a small bump, and either way you leave money on the table with a licensed professional standing right there. The right agent, one who has actually handled dated, needs-work senior homes, is worth tens of thousands.

## Choosing an Agent, and Evaluating Cash Offers

**Choosing an agent (traditional path), ask**

- How many senior transition sales have you handled?
- What is your average days on market in this price range?
- What repairs do you recommend, and why those?
- How will you keep disruption to the senior to a minimum?
- What is your commission, and is it negotiable?
- Can you give me references from similar situations?

**Evaluating a cash offer, do**

- Get offers from at least 2 to 3 cash buyers to compare
- Ask for proof of funds before you sign anything
- Ask directly whether they are the end investor or a wholesaler
- Compare net proceeds, not just the offer price
- Have your attorney review the contract before you sign
- Never accept an assignment clause without understanding it

## One Alternative Before You Sell

Selling is not the only way to reach the equity. If the goal is to stay in the home, a reverse mortgage (a HECM) can turn some of the equity into cash, with no monthly payment, while the owner lives there. It is not free money: it has fees, it reduces what heirs inherit, and it has to be weighed against simply selling. It can also collide with Medicaid if the cash sits unspent, and it usually comes due if the person later moves to care. If a move is likely within a couple of years, it is usually the wrong tool. Talk it through with a reverse-mortgage specialist and your financial advisor before you decide, never off a TV commercial.
