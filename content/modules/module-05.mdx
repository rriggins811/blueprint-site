## The Safety-First Walkthrough

Before you think about a single cosmetic upgrade, walk through the entire house with one question:

"What in this home could cause a fall, an injury, or an emergency?"

Falls are the #1 cause of injury-related death for adults over 65. One fall can change the entire transition from Stage 1 to Stage 3 overnight. Everything else, paint, curb appeal, staging, is secondary to keeping the senior safe while they still live in the home.

### Interior Safety Priorities

**Floors & Walkways:** Remove or secure all loose rugs (the single biggest trip hazard). Clear all hallways and walking paths of clutter, cords, and obstacles. Repair uneven flooring or thresholds. Ensure 36-inch minimum pathways for walker or wheelchair access.

**Lighting:** Replace every burnt-out bulb. Add nightlights in hallways, bathrooms, and between the bedroom and bathroom. Install motion-sensor lights in high-traffic areas. Stairs must be brightly and evenly lit with switches at both top and bottom.

**Stairs:** Install or secure handrails on both sides of all staircases. Add non-slip treads to each step. Mark the edge of the top and bottom steps with contrasting tape.

**Bathrooms:** Install grab bars near the toilet and inside the shower/tub (screwed into studs, not suction cups). Place non-slip mats inside and outside the shower. Consider a raised toilet seat. Remove bath mats that can bunch up and cause tripping.

**Kitchen:** Move heavy items to waist-level shelves (no reaching overhead or bending to floor cabinets). Remove items stored on top of the refrigerator. Ensure good lighting over the stove and sink.

### Exterior Safety Priorities

**Walkways & Steps:** Repair cracked or uneven sidewalks and porch steps. Add handrails to any exterior steps. Ensure outdoor lighting at all entry points. Trim bushes away from walkways.

**Driveway:** Fill potholes or cracks that could cause a stumble. Clear moss or algae that creates slippery surfaces.

### Emergency Systems

- Test all smoke detectors and replace batteries
- Test carbon monoxide detectors
- Post emergency numbers in a visible location by the phone
- Ensure the house number is clearly visible from the street for emergency responders
- Consider a medical alert system if the senior lives alone

## The Repair Priority System: Must-Fix, Should-Fix, Don't-Fix

Every repair in the house falls into one of three categories. Understanding this system will save you thousands of dollars and weeks of wasted time.

### MUST-FIX (Do This First)

Issues that affect safety, basic function, or a buyer's ability to get a mortgage. Lenders will not approve a loan on a house with active leaks, electrical hazards, or structural issues. If you don't fix these, you either can't sell or you're limited to cash buyers at steep discounts.

- Active roof leaks or water intrusion
- Electrical hazards (exposed wiring, overloaded panels, non-functional outlets)
- Broken or non-functional HVAC system
- Major plumbing issues (active leaks, failing water heater, sewer line problems)
- Rotted wood on exterior (fascia, soffits, deck, porch)
- Foundation cracks or structural concerns
- Mold or evidence of previous water damage

### SHOULD-FIX (Case by Case)

Low-cost, high-impact cosmetic items that improve buyer confidence. These are the "bang for your buck" repairs that cost $50-$500 each but make the home feel well-maintained.

- Wall scuffs, nail holes, and minor drywall damage
- Loose cabinet hardware and door handles
- Cracked or missing caulk around tubs, sinks, and windows
- Stained or yellowed light switch covers (replace for $1 each)
- Deep cleaning of all surfaces, especially kitchens and bathrooms
- Curb appeal items: mulch, trimmed bushes, swept porch, clean windows
- Fresh neutral paint in rooms that are scuffed, dark, or dated

### DON'T-FIX (Protect Your Money)

Expensive projects that rarely return their cost in a senior transition. This is where families waste the most money, usually because a well-meaning agent told them to "update" the house.

- Full kitchen remodel ($30K+), paint cabinets and update hardware instead ($1,700)
- Full bathroom remodel ($15K+), regrout, new fixtures, and paint instead ($1,600)
- Luxury flooring throughout ($15K+), clean carpets, only replace what's truly damaged
- Replacing functional appliances ($4K+), deep clean them until they sparkle
- Removing walls for "open concept" ($10K+), declutter and stage to create openness
- Adding a deck or patio ($8K+), clean and stain what's already there
- Landscaping overhaul ($5K+), mow, mulch, and trim for $300

**RYAN'S REAL TALK:** Here's the math that matters: a $1,500 cabinet painting job can add $5,000 in perceived value. A $30,000 kitchen remodel might add $15,000. The first one returns 233%. The second returns 50%. I've done both with my own money. I know which one I'd choose. Use Tool 5D (Repair Priority Assessment) to categorize every repair in your house before spending a dime.

## Why One Quote Turns You Into a Subtractor

There is a mechanic behind most bad repair decisions, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

A contractor hands you a single number. Nineteen thousand dollars. Now think about what question that leaves you. There is exactly one, and it has two answers: yes or no. One of those answers is scary and expensive and permanent. The other one is free and requires no courage from anybody.

Doing nothing always feels safe. So a lot of families take door number two, and it has nothing to do with whether the work needed doing.

But here is the part that costs real money. When somebody hands you one number, they have also handed you the job of making it smaller. And you will. Every time. You start looking for what can come out.

*What if we skipped the gutters?*
*Could we do just the back half?*
*What is the cheapest way to make this work?*

Notice what you are cutting: whatever feels optional. Not whatever kills the deal at inspection. Nobody looks at a single number and volunteers to cut the kitchen refresh and keep the roof, because the roof is invisible and the kitchen is the part you can picture. So the roof gets cut, and five months later a buyer's inspector finds it and re-trades your family for more than the repair would have cost.

You were never given a choice. You were given a target.

**The fix is to refuse the single number.** Ask any contractor to break the work into three buckets, priced separately: what an inspector will flag, what is unsafe, and what is cosmetic. That is the priority system above, and asking for it in that shape does two things at once. It stops you cutting blind, and it tells you a great deal about the contractor by how willingly they do it.

The same trap shows up when families pick an agent, which is covered in Module 09. One option and no ability to evaluate it means you participate by subtracting, or by deferring to whoever sounded most confident. Neither one is a decision.

## The 5 Biggest Money-Wasting Mistakes

These are the five mistakes I see most often. Every one of them is driven by good intentions and bad advice.

**Mistake 1: The Full Kitchen Remodel ($30K+).** Buyers in this price range often plan their own updates. Your expensive granite countertops and new cabinets might get ripped out within a year. Smart alternative: paint cabinets ($1,500), update hardware ($200), install new lighting ($500). You get 90% of the visual impact for 7% of the cost.

**Mistake 2: Luxury Flooring Throughout ($15K+).** Clean and presentable floors sell a house. Perfect floors don't add proportional value. Smart alternative: professionally clean all carpets ($400). Only replace carpet that's heavily stained, pet-damaged, or has visible wear paths.

**Mistake 3: Full Bathroom Remodel ($15K+).** A full gut is expensive, messy, and takes weeks. Smart alternative: regrout existing tile ($500), replace vanity ($700), install new faucet and lighting ($400), fresh paint. Feels brand new for $1,600.

**Mistake 4: Replacing Working Appliances ($4K+).** If the appliances function, leave them. Buyers are not paying a premium for new appliances in an older home. Smart alternative: deep clean every appliance until it sparkles. A $50 professional cleaning beats a $4,000 replacement.

**Mistake 5: Structural Changes for "Open Concept" ($10K+).** Permits required. Structural engineering potentially needed. Months of work. Smart alternative: remove bulky furniture, add strategic lighting, declutter aggressively. An empty room feels open. A cluttered room feels small regardless of the floor plan.

## The $5,000 Smart Prep Package

If you have a limited budget and want to maximize the return on every dollar, here's the highest-ROI $5,000 you can spend on any senior transition home:

**Deep Professional Cleaning ($400-$600):** Windows, carpets, kitchen, bathrooms, baseboards. This is the single highest-ROI item on the list. A clean house signals "well-maintained" to every buyer.

**Fresh Interior Paint in Neutral Colors ($2,000-$3,000):** The biggest visual transformation available. Neutral colors (Sherwin-Williams Agreeable Gray, Benjamin Moore Revere Pewter) make every room feel fresh and allow buyers to imagine their own style. Cover dark, dated, or scuffed walls.

**Carpet Cleaning or Targeted Replacement ($400-$1,000):** Professional carpet cleaning first. Only replace carpet in rooms where stains or damage can't be cleaned out. Budget carpet in a neutral color is fine, buyers know what they're getting.

**Minor Repairs ($500-$800):** Patch nail holes, fix loose hardware, replace broken light fixtures, repair dripping faucets, tighten wobbly railings. These are the details that make buyers feel confident the home has been cared for.

**Curb Appeal ($300-$500):** Fresh mulch in beds, trimmed bushes, power-washed walkways and exterior, a clean front door (or a $30 can of paint on it). First impressions happen in the first 7 seconds from the curb.

**Professional Staging Consultation ($200-$400):** Not full staging, just a consultation where a stager walks through and tells you how to arrange existing furniture and where to declutter. One visit with specific instructions you execute yourself.

Total: $3,800-$6,300, targeting the $5,000 sweet spot. These improvements typically return 2-3x their cost through faster sale time and higher offers.

## As-Is vs. Prepared Sale: Making the Right Call

One of the biggest strategic decisions in the entire transition is whether to prepare the home for a traditional listing or sell it as-is.

**Sell As-Is When:**

- You're in Stage 3 (urgent, 0-3 months) and speed matters more than price
- The home needs more than $15,000 in repairs
- You don't have the funds, time, or energy to manage contractors
- The senior has already moved and the house is sitting empty
- The emotional cost of managing repairs is too high for the family
- You want certainty, a guaranteed close date with no inspection surprises

**Prepare for Traditional Sale When:**

- You're in Stage 1-2 with 6+ months of runway
- The home needs less than $10,000 in cosmetic updates
- You have a local family member who can oversee contractors
- The home is in a desirable neighborhood where prep pays back
- You want to maximize the sale price

### The Hybrid Approach (Most Common)

Address only Must-Fix safety and functional issues. Do a deep clean. Handle the high-ROI cosmetic items from the $5,000 Smart Prep Package. Then price the home slightly below market to attract buyers who are willing to do their own cosmetic updates. This gives you most of the upside with less time, cost, and stress than a full preparation.

## Hiring and Managing Contractors

If you decide to make repairs, how you hire and manage contractors is the difference between staying on budget and getting taken for a ride. Here's how I managed contractors for 8+ years as an investor.

### The 3-Bid Rule

For any work over $1,000, get at least three written bids. Not verbal estimates, written bids with a detailed scope of work, timeline, and total cost. This protects you from overpaying and gives you room to negotiate.

### Red Flags (Walk Away Immediately)

- Demands more than 30% upfront before starting work
- No written contract or a vague "we'll figure it out as we go" scope
- No license or insurance (ask for proof, not just their word)
- Pressures you to "start today" or "lock in the price now"
- Won't provide references from recent similar jobs
- Wants to be paid in cash only
- Shows up without a company vehicle, business card, or professional appearance

### Best Practices

- Get a detailed written contract with scope, timeline, materials, and payment schedule
- Payment structure: 30% deposit, 30% at midpoint, 40% upon completion and inspection
- Never pay the final payment until all work is 100% complete and you've inspected it
- Take photos before, during, and after all work
- Check licenses at your state's licensing board website
- Ask for proof of general liability and workers' compensation insurance

**RYAN'S REAL TALK:** The most important thing I can teach you about contractors: the cheapest bid is rarely the best bid. Look for the bid with the most detailed scope of work. A contractor who takes the time to itemize everything is a contractor who's thought about the job. A contractor who gives you a one-line "$3,500 for bathroom work" hasn't. Use Tool 5C to compare bids side by side.

### Two the pros forget: the roof, and over-improving

First, watch the roof and the HVAC. A buyer cannot easily insure or finance a house with a failing roof, so that one issue can shrink your buyer pool and your price. Get real bids and decide on purpose, fix it before listing, or price for it. Second, do not over-improve. Spending $60,000 to chase a $30,000 bump is a classic loss, and grief and pressure push families into it every day. The goal is the handful of repairs that move the number, not a full remodel.
